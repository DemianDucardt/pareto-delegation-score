Day 1. Welcome to the job.

Orientation with Ivan Bunin plus the Pareto team. No experience needed.

Picture a hiring problem. A founder drowns in inbox plus calendar plus follow ups. A talented assistant wants real responsibility. Pareto Talent sits between them. It finds people, trains them for ten days, and places the best with founders as Right Hands.

The story starts in 2018. Kasim Aslam hired Ivan Bunin from a freelance site for 1,200 USD. Ivan grew from assistant to Tech Lead to CTO by 2022. Their agency Solutions 8 later sold for eight figures. Having lived both sides of hiring, the two founders started Pareto Talent. The first class graduated in April 2024.

Scale around it: more than 3,000 graduates across 6 bootcamps with more than 35,000 applicants all time. Of those, 350 were accepted to Pareto Talent. Money behind it: 1.2M USD paid in salary with 4.9 of 5 across 84 Google reviews. Model from Day 1 slide 20: We find talent and train them, then place them with entrepreneurs.

A Right Hand owns outcomes. An assistant completes tasks. The difference shows in a hard week: the Right Hand triages the inbox, guards calendar blocks, chases vendors, updates the CRM, writes meeting recaps, filters hiring mail, repurposes content, and ships a Friday report. Each task closes its own loop.

Day 2. How to talk to AI.

Craft day. Ivan: yesterday was the job, today is the craft.

Think of AI as a phone. Nobody studies chips and wiring. Everybody cares that taps respond. Ivan compresses thousands of practice hours into this class. Students dive in like Alice. The ones who use AI as professionals pull ahead, because companies are only starting to adopt it.

Context beats clever wording. Weak material in gives weak answers out. The class demo with random numbers proves models predict rather than calculate. So every task follows four moves:

  • Gather: pull raw material from the six daily sources.
  • Clean: keep first person lines plus numbers plus dates, drop filler.
  • Load: place files in the Project with instructions plus knowledge plus examples.
  • Ask: prompt against loaded context with a narrow job plus output shape.

Six daily sources named in class: YouTube plus Drive plus scraping plus Fathom plus dictation plus chat history. Slow typists gain the most here. Below 140 words per minute, install dictation. The fastest first bootcamp entry hit 90. The internal team runs Whisper Flow with 22 months Pro plus one free extra month. No page, video, or podcast stays unusable. Dictate its context instead.

The interview trick replaces blank page writing. Prompt the model to interview you with 20 questions, one by one, capped at 20, to build the thing. Ivan used it for a Facebook campaign plus a one pager partner doc plus a ClickUp SOP creator plus a free training offer for Argentina Right Hands. Answers pile into context. The final answer becomes the brief. Export whole threads, never single emails, and save as Markdown for reuse.

Day 3. Your second brain.

Storage day. Day 2 taught extraction. Day 3 adds memory plus agency.

Tuesday morning. A founder asks for the Q3 hiring rubric. You answer in four minutes. Nothing lived in your head. Everything lived in the folder. That is the whole pitch.

The idea predates the web. Niklas Luhmann wrote 90,000 paper slips across his career. A €5 million project spent 15 years digitizing them. A volunteer map later charted 73,715 cards as one network. Tiago Forte turned that lineage into PARA: Projects plus Areas plus Resources plus Archives. Four folders hold a life. Forte now teaches an AI Second Brain program built on the same four.

  • Context layer: Drive folders with stable names plus plain language docs plus MOC index.
  • Action layer: live connectors such as Gmail plus Drive scheduling plus sending.

PARA in practice, with real examples from the Forte Labs team. Projects carry deadlines: plan trip to the Netherlands plus hire a Social Media Writer plus update company website plus film a TV show pilot. Areas carry standards with no end date: apartment plus taxes. Resources carry fuel: email marketing plus mental models. Archives carry the closed past: former clients plus fellowship applications. A founder brain maps onto this shape: offer plus proof plus voice plus transcripts.

Atomicity decides note size. One idea per note, capped near 500 words. A single sentence counts. Each note carries a unique timestamp ID such as 202001151848, plus a header with tags, plus a body in your own words, plus backlinks to neighbors. Workflow runs four folders: capture fast in Fleeting, summarize sources in Literature, distill single ideas in Permanent, weave them on Index notes. Folders track process, with no sorting by topic inside them.

MOCs are the surfboards. MOC expands to Map of Content. A hub note titled Philosophy Overview links Ethics plus Metaphysics plus Existentialism plus Logic. Each child links deeper notes. The hub updates as the web grows. It never pretends to be final. Digital vaults cannot stack similar cards side by side, so the MOC restores that adjacency.

Research supplement, labeled: retrieval science behind the brain. Production AI pairs a retriever with a generator, with fusion logic between them. Documents split into chunks, each chunk embedded as a vector, queries matched by similarity, top matches fused into the answer. Chunking styles tested in papers: fixed length plus semantic plus content based plus adaptive. Finding with practical bite: metadata as text, such as prefixing title plus owner plus date plus type, lifts retrieval accuracy, though index rebuilds cost compute. Student translation: file names plus titles plus tags plus frontmatter decide what Claude finds. Messy names bury good notes.
Research supplement, labeled: connectors behind the action layer. MCP docs compare the standard to a USB-C port for AI applications. Servers expose actions the model can call plus data it can read. Day 5. Offers before pixels.

Public page day. A landing page is an offer. Friday session ran 2 hours 11 minutes.

Day 5 turns the week into a URL. Context plus Second Brain plus Skills plus Routines become something to point at. Ivan states the order in the first 90 seconds. Students already hold almost everything for a full website with AI. A Brain speaking as Kasim Aslam changes output quality immediately.

Offer comes before pixels. Logic follows Alex Hormozi Grand Slam Offer: dream outcome plus likelihood plus speed plus ease with risk removed by guarantee. Never write cold. Slide instruction: ask Claude to interview you for each card with 20 questions for a perfect offer.

Five cards. Slide text with warnings.

  • 01 Audience. Who this is actually for, specifically. Name the exact person. Business stage. Role. What a normal week actually looks like for them. Slide warns against everyone who wants to grow.
  • 02 Problem. The one pain they would genuinely pay to make go away. Single pain keeping them up at night right now, in their own words. Slide warns against a list of five problems.
  • 03 Solution. What you are actually offering to fix it. The real outcome they walk away with once it is working. Slide warns against tool or feature talk.
  • 04 Bonuses. What sweetens the deal beyond the core solution. Real value that knocks out their next objection. Slide warns against random extras bolted on to pad the offer.
  • 05 Guarantee. What removes the risk from their side, with no shift to yours. If it does not work, say plainly what happens to their money or their time.

Idea extracted from Ivan talk: most entrepreneurs start and end with solution. Example given in class: I will do websites, with no stated for whom, no pain, no outcome. That is why offers fail. As a Right Hand, packaging offers into websites and funnels is core work. Ivan repeated the thesis twice: every section exists to fight one objection, offer first, page second. Worked example with course numbers: dream outcome reads 12.5 hours back weekly. Likelihood rides on 100 plus founders plus 93 percent retention. Delay shrinks to a 2 minute quiz with 3 matches in 24 hours. Effort drops to reply by email.
